Chapter 3
Research Gap
This chapter presents a rigorous, evidence-based identification of the five research gaps that collectively define the original contribution of this thesis. Each gap is grounded in specific findings from the literature review (Chapter 2), substantiated by a structured evidence table naming the papers responsible for the gap, and mapped forward to the research objective that closes it. The gaps are not incremental improvements over prior work — each represents a structural absence in the current state of the art: a capability that every surveyed system either explicitly lacks or has never attempted.

3.1  Gap Identification Methodology
Research gaps in this thesis were identified through a three-stage systematic process:
Stage 1 — Capability inventory: Each of the six primary systems reviewed in Chapter 2 (Farmer.Chat, AgriDoctor, AgriGPT, AgriM-LLM, AgroAskAI, MDPI Agentic) was mapped against seven capability dimensions: multi-agent routing, vision-based disease diagnosis, soil sensor fusion, explainability to farmer, voice/Indic language support, live market price advisory, and multi-crop Indian deployment. This produced a binary capability matrix (Table 2.3).
Stage 2 — Column-wise gap detection: Each capability dimension was inspected across all six systems. A gap was declared when no system achieved the capability — or when every system achieving partial capability had a documented, specific limitation that prevented full deployment-readiness.
Stage 3 — Impact quantification: Each gap was connected to a documented agricultural outcome (yield loss, adoption failure, revenue loss, accessibility barrier) to establish that closing the gap has measurable real-world significance beyond academic novelty.

This process identified five primary research gaps (G1–G5). These are presented below in order of architectural centrality — from the broadest system-level gap (G1) to the most specific integration gap (G5). 3.2  G1 — No Unified Multimodal Advisory Session

G1  No Unified Multimodal Advisory Session
Statement of gap
No existing system combines disease diagnosis (image input), nutrition deficiency advisory (soil sensor input), crop recommendation (structured tabular input), weather and irrigation scheduling (API data input), and live market price advisory (real-time feed) within a single conversational session. A farmer who asks — in one message — 'my tomato leaves are yellowing, what should I do and should I sell now?' cannot receive a unified, coherent, source-grounded response from any single deployed system today.
Evidence from literature
Farmer.Chat (ACM, 2024) handles text and knowledge-base queries only — no image, sensor, or market input. AgriDoctor (IEEE, 2025) routes multimodal inputs to disease and pest components only — four of the five advisory domains are absent. AgriGPT (arXiv, 2025) achieves strong text-based RAG advisory but has no image diagnosis, no sensor integration, and no market advisory. AgroAskAI (arXiv, 2024) covers climate advisory in isolation. The MDPI Agentic system (2026) covers three domains (soil, weather, rice disease) but only for a single crop and without RAG grounding or market integration. No system covers all five domains simultaneously.
Why this gap matters — agricultural impact
Agricultural decisions are inherently interdependent. The decision to treat a crop for disease cannot be divorced from the decision of whether to harvest early or treat and wait — which depends on the current mandi price. Fertiliser recommendations are only actionable when paired with a sowing calendar adjusted for the current weather forecast. Existing systems force farmers to seek advisory from multiple disconnected sources, introducing inconsistency, delay, and decision fatigue. The ICAR extension model is designed around integrated advisory — the proposed system replicates this integration digitally.
Closest prior attempt and its remaining limitation
The MDPI Agentic framework (Scientific Reports, 2026) comes closest, with four agents covering soil monitoring, weather, rice disease detection, and a supervisor chatbot. However, it covers only one crop (rice), only three domains, has no RAG knowledge grounding, no market advisory, no voice input, and no XAI. It demonstrates that multi-agent agricultural chatbots are feasible — but not that a unified five-domain system has been built.
Research objective that closes this gap
O1 — Design a LangGraph-based orchestrator that classifies intent and routes inputs to five specialist agents in parallel, merging responses into a single coherent advisory reply with multi-turn session memory.

3.3  G2 — No Cross-Modal Soil Sensor and Leaf Image Fusion for Nutrition Advisory in a Conversational System

G2  No Cross-Modal Sensor + Image Nutrition Fusion in a Dialogue
Statement of gap
No existing system fuses real-time soil sensor readings (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, pH, electrical conductivity) with leaf image features within a conversational advisory interface to diagnose nutrient deficiency and generate a fertiliser dose recommendation. Both modalities exist in isolation — vision models diagnose disease from images, and rule-based systems recommend fertiliser from soil NPK values — but no system combines them cross-modally within a live, farmer-accessible dialogue.
Evidence from literature
AgriM-LLM (Preprints, 2026) represents the state of the art in agricultural vision encoding — its Multi-Scale Feature Fusion encoder captures subtle disease symptoms from leaf images, but soil sensor data is entirely absent from its input. The system produces disease and pest identification only; nutrient deficiency requires both visual symptom recognition (yellowing patterns, interveinal chlorosis, tip burn) and soil measurement correlation (which nutrient is actually deficient) — AgriM-LLM addresses only one half. Cross-modal fusion papers in plant science (PMC, 2025) have demonstrated that combining image and sensor data improves nutrition diagnosis accuracy by 23% over image-only approaches — but these systems operate as offline inference pipelines with no conversational interface, no farmer-accessible output, and no RAG-grounded treatment recommendation.
Why this gap matters — agricultural impact
The Soil Health Card scheme has issued 230 million cards to Indian farmers, yet only 14.6% acted on the recommendations (Ministry of Agriculture, 2023). The primary reason identified in field studies is that farmers cannot translate NPK numbers on paper into actionable fertiliser dosage without agronomist support. Simultaneously, visual nutrient deficiency symptoms — yellowing leaves, stunted growth, purple discolouration — are highly ambiguous: identical symptoms can result from nitrogen deficiency, iron deficiency, or certain fungal infections. Cross-modal fusion resolves this ambiguity by jointly analysing the visual symptom pattern and the soil chemistry, enabling confident diagnosis and precise dosage output in the farmer's language.
Closest prior attempt and its remaining limitation
The MDPI Agentic framework (2026) deploys Agent A for soil NPK monitoring and Agent C for rice disease detection — but these operate as separate agents with no cross-modal fusion. Agent A does not use leaf image features; Agent C does not use soil readings. No fusion network integrates both modalities for nutrition deficiency specifically. The proposed system introduces a dedicated cross-modal MLP that concatenates ResNet-50 leaf image feature vectors with normalised soil sensor readings into a unified representation for deficiency classification and dose prediction — a design that no prior agricultural AI system implements.
Research objective that closes this gap
O3 — Develop a cross-modal fusion network combining ResNet-50 leaf image features with real-time NPK/pH/EC sensor readings to generate fertiliser dose recommendations aligned with ICAR Soil Health Card norms, deployed within the conversational advisory loop.

3.4  G3 — Explainable AI Never Rendered to the Farmer in Any Deployed System

G3  XAI Never Rendered to the Farmer in Any Deployed Agricultural AI System
Statement of gap
No deployed agricultural AI system — chatbot, mobile application, or web platform — renders visual explainability outputs (Grad-CAM saliency heatmaps, SHAP feature importance charts) directly to the farmer as part of the advisory response. Explainability techniques are extensively used in agricultural AI research as internal quality-control tools for model developers and as evaluation metrics for researchers, but no system has completed the critical last step: putting the explanation in the farmer's hands at the point of advice delivery.
Evidence from literature
XAI-CROP (Springer NCA, 2024) applies SHAP TreeExplainer to XGBoost crop recommendation models and generates feature importance charts as research output — the paper explicitly presents them as researcher-facing model validation tools, not as farmer-facing UI components. LLMI-CDP (Scientific Reports, 2025) generates Grad-CAM saliency maps during evaluation to verify that the disease model attends to the correct leaf region — again as a quality-control mechanism, not as output to the farmer. AgriDoctor (IEEE, 2025) produces classification confidence scores but provides no visual attribution. The Agentic XAI paper (arXiv, 2025) explicitly generates SHAP-based explanations for crop scientists evaluated on seven technical quality metrics — farmer readability is not a design criterion. Across all 25+ papers reviewed, not a single system renders a Grad-CAM overlay or SHAP chart in a farmer-accessible interface.
The trust and adoption evidence
XAI-CROP (2024) provides the strongest empirical evidence that this gap has real consequences: farmers shown SHAP feature bars alongside crop recommendations adopted the AI advice 31% more frequently than those shown recommendations without explanation. A parallel finding from Farmer.Chat (ACM, 2024) notes that even with 565,000 users, adoption of AI-generated advisory recommendations that cannot be verified by the farmer remains below 60%. The consistent finding across agricultural AI adoption literature is that farmers — particularly smallholder farmers with limited formal education — require the ability to interrogate AI reasoning before trusting actionable recommendations. Visual XAI provides exactly this capability.
Closest prior attempt and its remaining limitation
The Agentic XAI framework (arXiv, 2025) comes closest by iteratively refining SHAP explanations through LLM-driven rounds until agronomist-rated quality reaches a threshold. However, this system targets crop scientists — its explanations contain technical notation, numerical SHAP values, and crop science terminology inappropriate for low-literacy farmers. The language, visual format, and assumed background knowledge all differ from what is required for a farmer-facing interface. The proposed system adapts the concept of iterative explanation refinement but constrains output to two farmer-readable visualisation types: Grad-CAM colour heatmaps overlaid on the farmer's uploaded leaf photo, and horizontal SHAP bar charts rendered in Hindi-labelled axes.
Research objective that closes this gap
O5 — Surface Grad-CAM saliency heatmaps for disease diagnosis and SHAP feature importance bar charts for crop recommendation directly within the React PWA advisory response — as a farmer-facing UI component, not a research output.

3.5  G4 — No End-to-End Indic Voice-to-Advisory Pipeline in Any Agricultural System

G4  No End-to-End Indic Voice-to-Advisory Pipeline
Statement of gap
No agricultural AI system has implemented a complete, end-to-end pipeline that accepts a farmer's voice query in Hindi or any other Indic language, processes it through an ASR model, routes it to a RAG-grounded domain advisory backend, and delivers the response as spoken audio via TTS — within a single, integrated system. Each component of this pipeline exists independently in the literature; their integration into a unified agricultural advisory voice interface has not been achieved in any prior work.
Evidence from literature
The Indic ASR Benchmark (arXiv, 2025) provides the strongest evidence that the technology is ready: Whisper large-v3 achieves WER 16.2% for Hindi agricultural speech on 10,934 real farm voice recordings. The benchmark explicitly concludes that ASR performance for Hindi is production-grade — but the paper itself contains no advisory backend. Farmer.Chat (ACM, 2024) integrates Bhashini ASR for voice input — the only deployed system to do so — but voice input routes to a text-based RAG pipeline with text output only; no TTS response delivery is implemented, and Bhashini coverage extends partially to only two or three Indic languages in the deployment. AgriDoctor (IEEE, 2025) is Chinese-English only with no voice input. AgriGPT (arXiv, 2025) is text-only. AgroAskAI (arXiv, 2024) claims multilingual support but provides no ASR or TTS implementation details.
Why this gap matters — accessibility impact
India has a rural literacy rate of approximately 67.8% (Census 2021), with substantially lower rates among women and older farmers in agricultural states. Text-based interfaces — the modality used by all prior systems — structurally exclude this population. The NSSO Agricultural Survey (2019) found that 78% of farmers aged 50+ in UP, Bihar, and MP had never sent a text message. Voice is not a preference for these farmers — it is the only viable interaction modality. The linguistic diversity compounds this: Hindi alone cannot serve Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra, West Bengal, or Punjab — states that together account for over 40% of India's cultivated area. An agricultural AI system that cannot speak to farmers in their language is not an agricultural AI system for India.
The pipeline gap — three connected components, never assembled
The gap is specifically an integration gap, not a component gap. Component 1 (Indic ASR): Whisper large-v3 fine-tuned on agricultural speech achieves production-grade WER. Component 2 (Multilingual RAG): Hindi-English bilingual knowledge indices over ICAR documents can be built with E5-large-v2 multilingual embeddings. Component 3 (Indic TTS): Bhashini API delivers production-quality TTS for 10+ Indic languages. What has never been done is assembling these three components into a single pipeline where a farmer speaks into a phone, the system understands, reasons from verified agronomic knowledge, and speaks the advisory response back — in the same language, within four seconds.
Research objective that closes this gap
O6 — Implement an end-to-end voice advisory pipeline using Whisper large-v3 (Indic fine-tuned) for ASR and Bhashini TTS for response delivery, targeting WER < 20% for Hindi and < 30% for five regional languages, with end-to-end latency < 4 seconds.

3.6  G5 — Live Market Price Advisory Never Co-Delivered with Agronomic Advice

G5  Live Market Price Never Co-Delivered with Agronomic Advisory
Statement of gap
No existing agricultural AI system integrates live mandi price data, price forecasting, and sell/hold advisory within the same conversational session as agronomic advice (disease diagnosis, crop recommendation, nutrition advisory). Market intelligence and agronomic advisory exist in completely separate silos — different platforms, different data sources, different interaction paradigms — despite the fact that a farmer's actual decision requires both simultaneously.
Evidence from literature
Across all six systems reviewed in Chapter 2, market price is uniformly absent. Farmer.Chat (ACM, 2024) covers crop management and pest advisory — no market data. AgriDoctor (IEEE, 2025) covers disease and pest identification — no market data. AgriGPT (arXiv, 2025) — the most comprehensive knowledge ecosystem reviewed — explicitly notes market price forecasting as a future work direction, confirming it is not yet present. AgroLLM (arXiv, 2025) builds a domain knowledge pipeline for crop management with no market integration. The MDPI Agentic framework (2026) covers soil, weather, and disease — no market agent. Agmarknet and eNAM exist as standalone government platforms accessible only to farmers with digital literacy and awareness of the specific websites, with no conversational interface, no AI advisory layer, and no integration with agronomic data.
Why this gap matters — economic impact
The decision to treat a disease and hold the crop, or harvest early and sell before further loss, is simultaneously an agronomic decision and a market decision. It cannot be made correctly with agronomic data alone. eNAM Annual Report (2023) documents that farmers sell 30–40% below optimal price due to: (1) selling at distress timing without price trend awareness, (2) travelling to the nearest mandi without knowing that a mandi 30 km further offers 15% higher price, and (3) having no forecast of whether prices will rise or fall over the next seven days. These are precisely the outputs a market advisory agent can deliver — but only if it shares a conversational session with the agronomic advisor, so that the two recommendations are synthesised into a single actionable answer.
The compound query problem
Real farmer queries are compound. 'My tomato has a disease, should I treat it or sell the crop now?' cannot be answered by a disease agent alone. It requires: (1) disease identification and severity assessment, (2) current tomato price at the nearest mandi, (3) price trend over the next seven days, (4) estimated crop loss if untreated vs treatment cost, (5) MSP comparison. Only a system with both a disease agent and a market agent operating in the same session — with a response-merging orchestrator — can synthesise these inputs into a coherent advisory answer. This is G5's core contribution.
Research objective that closes this gap
O7 — Integrate Agmarknet and eNAM live mandi price feeds (15-minute refresh) with a Prophet-based 7-day price forecast model to co-deliver sell, hold, or transport-to-mandi advisory within the same conversational session as agronomic advice.

3.8  Significance of the Identified Gaps
The five identified gaps are significant for two reasons beyond their academic novelty. First, each gap has a quantified agricultural impact — a documented real-world consequence of the gap remaining open, measured in yield loss, adoption failure, or revenue loss. Second, the gaps are structurally interconnected: G1 (no unified session) is the architectural gap that the other four gaps compound. G2, G3, G4, and G5 each represent a specific capability that is absent from the unified session — so closing G1 without closing the others would still produce an incomplete system.

Together these five gaps represent the complete set of structural deficiencies that prevent any existing agricultural AI system from functioning as a deployment-ready, trustworthy, and accessible advisory solution for Indian smallholder farmers. The proposed research addresses all five gaps simultaneously — a design choice that is not merely additive but architecturally necessary: a system that closes G1 but not G3 is untrustworthy; a system that closes G4 but not G2 excludes sensor-equipped farms; a system that closes all four but not G5 delivers incomplete advisory for the majority of real farmer queries.
